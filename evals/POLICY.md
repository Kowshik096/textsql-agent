# Benchmark semantic policy

Decisions that apply to every reference query. They exist because the same
question has several defensible readings, and an unstated choice becomes a
silent, unfalsifiable assumption baked into the ground truth.

Day 1 proved why this matters: the first generated query summed
`price + freight_value` and counted cancelled orders. Both were wrong. Neither
errored. The numbers looked plausible and the top-5 seller ranking was
materially different.

| # | Decision | Chosen | Rejected alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Revenue | `SUM(price)` | including `freight_value` (shipping cost, not seller earnings) |
| 2 | Revenue status filter | exclude `canceled`, `unavailable` | counting all statuses |
| 3 | Date basis | `order_purchase_timestamp` | `order_delivered_customer_date` — NULL on cancelled/undelivered, so it silently shrinks every date-filtered result |
| 4 | "How many orders" | `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)` | `COUNT(*)` on `order_items` — counts line items, inflating by ~13% |
| 5 | Scope of the status filter | money questions only | all questions — would make "how many orders were cancelled" return 0 |

Decisions 1 and 2 were settled on Day 1 and are encoded in `COLUMN_NOTES` in
`src/db.py`, so the model is told about them too. Decisions 3, 4 and 5 were
settled before writing the benchmark.

## Why reference SQL is stored, not just expected numbers

Each case carries the query that produces the answer, and expected results are
generated by running it. That keeps expectations regenerable if the database is
rebuilt, and puts the human verification effort where it belongs — on reviewing
whether the SQL means what the question asks — rather than on transcribing
numbers by hand.
